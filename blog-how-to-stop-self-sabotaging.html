How to Stop Self-Sabotaging (Especially When Things Are Going Well)

By Freddy Owen·October 2026

I've done this. Most people reading this have done this. You're finally getting some momentum — the job is going well, the relationship feels solid, your habits are clicking — and then you do something that blows it up. You pick an unnecessary fight. You slack off at exactly the wrong moment. You drink too much at an important event. You pull away from someone who was getting close.

And then you sit there afterward wondering what the hell is wrong with you.

Here's what's actually going on: you're not broken, and you're not stupid. You're doing something your nervous system learned to do, and it's backfiring. Understanding that isn't an excuse — it's the starting point for actually stopping it.

What Self-Sabotage Actually Is

Self-sabotage is any behavior that works against your own goals and interests. But the key word is behavior — this isn't about bad luck or external circumstances. It's something you're doing, consciously or not, that undermines what you say you want.

The reason it's so confusing is that self-sabotage almost never feels like self-destruction in the moment. It usually feels like relief, control, or just a completely reasonable decision. You're not thinking "I'm going to ruin this." You're thinking "I just need a drink to take the edge off" or "I just need some space" or "I'm just being honest about what I really think of this." The sabotage is the action. The intention, in the moment, feels neutral or even reasonable.

That's what makes it hard to catch.

The 5 Most Common Forms of Self-Sabotage

1. Procrastination on things that matter

Not everyday procrastination on things you don't care about — this is specifically dragging your feet on things that are important to you. The opportunity is there. The path is clear. And you find every possible reason not to take the next step. If you've been stuck in a rut that you can't explain, procrastination-as-sabotage is worth examining.

2. Creating conflict in relationships at key moments

Things are getting serious with someone, or a friendship is deepening, and you suddenly find something to be angry about. You pick a fight over something minor. You say the thing you know will hurt them. This isn't necessarily conscious — but it has the effect of creating distance right when closeness feels risky.

3. Numbing with substances or distractions

Drinking, scrolling, gaming, overeating — these aren't inherently bad, but when they consistently show up right before or after important moments, they're functioning as avoidance. The question isn't whether you drink. It's whether you drink to make the good thing feel less real, or to dull the anxiety that comes with having something to lose.

4. Underperforming right before a breakthrough

You've been building momentum, the thing you've been working toward is finally within reach — and you start cutting corners, missing workouts, phoning it in at work. It looks like laziness. It's actually fear wearing laziness as a disguise.

5. Catastrophizing success

This one is sneaky. You start imagining everything that could go wrong if this actually works out. The responsibility. The expectations. The possibility of eventually losing it. And so you start to unconsciously steer away from the very thing you've been working toward, because losing it later feels worse than not having it now.

Why Self-Sabotage Spikes When Things Are Going Well

This is the part that trips people up. Logically, you'd expect self-sabotage to be worst when things are bad. But many people find it's actually worst during good periods. Why?

Because when things are going well, you have more to lose.

Your brain does a constant risk calculation. When you're at rock bottom, the downside of failure is manageable — you're already there. When you're succeeding, the downside of failure is much higher. That gap between where you are and where you could fall to is terrifying, even if you're not consciously aware of it.

Self-sabotage is your brain trying to eliminate the anxiety of potential loss by controlling the outcome — even if that means being the one to bring the good thing down yourself.

There's also the factor of deserving. A lot of self-sabotage is rooted in a belief — often unconscious, often formed early in life — that good things don't last for people like you, or that you don't deserve to keep what you've earned. This isn't something you can think your way out of quickly. But naming it is the first step.

How to Catch Yourself Before You Do It

The hardest part about stopping self-sabotage is that by the time you realize you've done it, you've already done it. So the real work is in recognizing the early signals — the triggers that precede the behavior.

Map your pattern backwards

Think about the last time you clearly sabotaged something. Now work backwards: what was happening in the week before it? What was the emotional state right before the behavior? What thought or feeling preceded the action? You're looking for your personal trigger — the internal state that precedes your specific flavor of self-sabotage.

For a lot of people, it's a feeling of things being "too good" or "too much" — an anxiety that's hard to name but feels like they're waiting for the other shoe to drop. That feeling is the trigger. Once you can name the trigger, you can build a response to it that isn't sabotage.

Tell someone your pattern

This works partly because naming the pattern out loud to another person makes it real in a way that internal awareness doesn't always achieve. It also creates accountability. If your partner, close friend, or mentor knows "when things are going well, I tend to pick fights or pull away," they can name it when they see it happening in real time — before you've done too much damage.

Buy yourself a 24-hour window

When you notice you're about to do the thing — send the text that starts a fight, cancel the meeting, skip the workout — give yourself 24 hours before you act. This isn't "just don't do it." It's "give yourself time to be honest about what's driving the impulse." In a lot of cases, 24 hours is enough to interrupt the loop. You can still do the thing tomorrow if you genuinely want to. But usually you won't.

How to Build a Pattern Interrupt

A pattern interrupt is a specific, concrete action you take the moment you notice the trigger. The goal isn't to suppress the feeling — it's to insert a gap between the trigger and the behavior so that you have the option to choose differently.

Your interrupt needs to be:

  • Specific: not "I'll think about it" but "I will go for a 15-minute walk immediately"
  • Physical if possible: movement interrupts the emotional loop more reliably than thinking does
  • Pre-decided: you decide what the interrupt is in a calm moment, not in the middle of the spiral
  • Simple enough to actually do: a complicated interrupt won't survive contact with a strong urge

Good examples: go for a walk, call a specific person, write in a journal for 10 minutes, do 20 push-ups, leave the room and make tea. Bad examples: "meditate for an hour" or "review my values document." Keep it simple. The goal is interruption, not a full therapeutic process.

Dealing With the Deeper Layer

The interrupts and the pattern-mapping are real tools, and they work. But they work on the surface of self-sabotage. The deeper layer — the beliefs about what you deserve, what's sustainable for you, what happens when things go well — those require more work, often with a therapist, sometimes through sustained honest reflection over months.

That's not a reason not to start with the surface tools. You can interrupt the behavior while the deeper work is happening. In fact, changing the behavior is part of changing the belief — every time you don't self-sabotage when you'd normally do it, you're building evidence that a different story is possible.

If this pattern keeps coming up around how you handle yourself under pressure, it's worth reading more about how to genuinely change your mindset — not the fluffy version, but the kind that actually sticks.

You are not broken. You are a person with a pattern that isn't serving you. Patterns can be changed — but only if you're willing to be honest enough to see them clearly first.

The Bottom Line

If you self-sabotage, you're in good company. It's one of the most common and least talked-about patterns in human behavior. The fact that it most often strikes when things are going well is not ironic — it's logical, given what your nervous system is trying to protect you from.

But understanding it doesn't mean you have to accept it. You can map the pattern, name the trigger, build a concrete interrupt, and tell someone you trust. You can do those things starting today, before you've figured out every deep psychological root of why it happens.

The self-sabotage will not stop on its own. But it can stop. And it stops the same way most patterns stop: one interrupt, one choice, one small act of not doing the thing you'd normally do — repeated until a different path becomes more familiar than the old one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I self-sabotage when things are going well?

When things are going well, you have more to lose. Your brain interprets this increased stake as increased risk, and triggers self-protective behavior — even when that behavior works against you. It's also often tied to a belief that you don't deserve good things, or a fear that you won't be able to sustain the success. The sabotage feels like control when the future feels uncertain.

What are signs of self-sabotaging behavior?

Common signs include: procrastinating on things that matter, creating conflict in relationships right when they're getting serious, drinking or using substances when you're under positive pressure, avoiding people or situations that could lead to success, making excuses not to follow through on commitments, and picking fights or creating drama when life is going smoothly. If you notice a pattern of things falling apart right when they were going well, self-sabotage is worth examining.

Is self-sabotage a mental health issue?

Self-sabotage itself is a behavior pattern, not a diagnosis. But it's often associated with anxiety, low self-worth, depression, or past trauma. If self-sabotage is a persistent pattern that's significantly affecting your life and you can't seem to interrupt it on your own, working with a therapist — especially one trained in cognitive-behavioral approaches — can be genuinely useful. It's not a personality defect; it's a learnable pattern that can be unlearned.

How do I stop sabotaging my relationships and goals?

Start by identifying your specific pattern — what does your self-sabotage usually look like? Then work backwards from the behavior to the trigger: what was happening right before you did the thing that undermined you? Build a concrete interrupt: a specific action you take the moment you notice the trigger. Tell someone you trust about the pattern so they can call it out when they see it. And work on the underlying belief that's driving it — usually a belief about what you deserve or what's sustainable for you.
