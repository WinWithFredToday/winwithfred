How to Stop Being Late (And Actually Show Up On Time)

By Freddy Owen · September 4, 2026 · 10 min read

You said seven. You left at seven. And on the way over you ran the maths you always run — if the lights are kind, if there is a space outside, if you walk fast — and you arrived at seven fifteen with the apology already loaded.

Nobody said much. They never do. That is part of the problem.

And you have promised yourself you will figure out how to be on time roughly four hundred times.

If you want to know how to stop being late, you have to start somewhere other than "care more." You already care. That is why your stomach drops in the car. Chronic lateness is almost never a caring problem. It is an arithmetic problem that other people have quietly filed under character flaw.

This post covers three things: why your brain keeps handing you the wrong number, the hidden minutes that destroy every plan you make, and a way of working backwards that takes the guesswork out entirely. No arriving an hour early. No setting your watch fast and then mentally subtracting. Just a method that makes on time the default instead of the exception.

Chronic Lateness Is Not Disrespect — But It Reads Like It

Start with the uncomfortable half. Whatever is actually going on in your head, the message other people receive is simple: my time is worth more than yours.

They do not see the six things you did before leaving. They see a chair that was empty when it should not have been. And because chronic lateness repeats, they stop reading it as an accident and start reading it as information about you.

That is what makes it expensive. Not the fifteen minutes — the reputation. People stop giving you the early slot. They stop telling you the real start time. They give the presentation to someone else because they cannot risk you walking in halfway through.

You lose things you never find out you lost.

Now the other half, which matters just as much: you are not doing it on purpose. Almost nobody is. If lateness were really about arrogance, you would not feel the dread that shows up around minute forty of getting ready, when you first suspect you are not going to make it and start moving faster instead of leaving.

Both things are true. Your intentions are fine and your impact is not. So learning how to stop being late is not a matter of defending the intention harder. It is a matter of changing the impact, which is a mechanical job. Same reason keeping promises to yourself comes down to system design rather than sincerity.

You do not need to become a different person to be on time. You need to stop using an estimate that has been wrong for twenty years.

Why Your Brain Keeps Giving You the Wrong Number

Ask a chronically late person how long the drive takes and you will get a real answer, delivered with confidence. It will also be wrong — and wrong in one direction, every time.

Here is why. When you estimate a journey, you do not remember the average one. You remember the best one. The Sunday morning run with no traffic. The day you found parking outside. Your brain stores the fastest version and quietly files it as normal.

Then it does something worse. It gives you a number for the driving and nothing at all for the rest.

The planning fallacy, in plain English

Psychologists call this the planning fallacy: we underestimate how long our own tasks will take, even when we have done the exact same task a hundred times and been late a hundred times. Evidence does not correct it. That is the strange bit. You can be late to the same meeting weekly for a year and still believe, on the way out the door, that today is the day twenty minutes will be enough.

This is what people mean by time blindness — not that you cannot read a clock, but that your internal estimate of how long things take does not update from experience. If you have ever thought "that cannot possibly have taken forty minutes" while looking at a clock that says it did, you know the feeling.

The good news: you do not have to fix the estimate. You just have to stop using it. Replace the guess with a measurement and the whole thing collapses.

For one week, time three routine journeys door to door. Not the driving. Door to door, from the moment you stand up to the moment you are actually where you are meant to be. Write the real numbers down. Most people find the gap is somewhere between ten and twenty-five minutes, consistently, every single time.

That number is not a personality trait. It is your correction factor, and you now have it in writing.

How to Stop Being Late by Planning Backwards

Almost everyone plans forwards. You look at the clock, look at the task, and think: I have got time. Then you fill the time, and the leaving happens whenever the filling runs out.

Backwards planning inverts it. You do not start from now. You start from the moment you need to be sitting down, and you walk backwards through every step until you land on a departure time. That time is fixed. Everything else has to fit around it.

Try it out loud once and you will see the problem immediately.

Meeting at 9:00. Need to be sitting at 8:55. Walk from the car park, five minutes, so parked by 8:50. Parking is a coin flip, give it eight, so arrive at 8:42. Drive is twenty-two on a good day, twenty-eight on a normal one, so leave at 8:14. Shoes, keys, coat, the bit where you cannot find your phone — that is six minutes minimum. Stop what you are doing at 8:08.

Not 8:30, which is what you have been telling yourself. 8:08.

The dead minutes nobody counts

The gap between 8:08 and 8:30 is almost entirely made of steps that feel too small to count. Finding keys. Locking up. The last message you have to send. Deciding on a jacket. Walking to the car. The petrol light. The thing you go back inside for.

None of these feel like real time. Together they are usually the whole of your lateness.

This is where time blindness does its real damage — not on the motorway, but in the four minutes you spend deciding on a jacket. So count them. Write the actual chain for the journey you are late to most often, one line per step, with a number next to each. Add it up. That total, not your instinct, is now your leaving time. You only have to do this once per recurring journey — the work is in the first ten minutes and then you have it forever.

This is the same principle behind any decent system for managing your time: stop negotiating with a fresh estimate every single day, and make the decision once when you are calm.

Set the Alarm for Leaving, Not for Arriving

If there is one lever that does the most work in learning how to stop being late, it is this one, and it takes about thirty seconds to set up.

Most people set a reminder for the event. Meeting at nine, alarm at nine, or maybe ten minutes before if they are being careful. But you do not need to know when the meeting starts. You already know. What you need is a hard interrupt at the moment you have to physically stop doing whatever you are doing.

So move the alarm. Label it with the action, not the appointment: "LEAVE NOW — Dentist" at 8:08. Not "Dentist 9:00."

The label matters more than it should. An alarm that names a time gets negotiated with. An alarm that names an action gets obeyed, because there is nothing to interpret.

Two rules make it stick.

First, the alarm is not information. It is a command. When it goes, you stand up mid-sentence, mid-email, mid-anything. The email will survive. This is the entire discipline — there is no other step.

Second, put a second alarm ten minutes earlier labelled "WRAP UP." Late people are rarely ambushed by the clock. They are mid-task and cannot bear to leave it unfinished, so they steal four minutes, then four more. The wrap-up alarm gives you a place to stop that is not a cliff edge.

If you want this to hold on the mornings that go wrong, it has to live inside something you already do. That is where a real morning routine earns its keep — not the cold plunges, just a fixed order of operations so the first decision of the day is not being made at 8:04.

Deal With the One Thing That Always Makes You Late

Be honest for a second. There is usually one culprit.

For some people it is the phone — a quick check at 7:50 that ends at 8:11. For others it is the last task, the one that would only take a minute and never does. For others it is a shared bathroom, a school run, or one child who cannot find one shoe. And for a lot of people it is the belief that leaving early is wasted time, so they optimise the departure down to the last possible second and lose the coin flip roughly half the time.

Name yours. Then move it, do not fight it.

If it is the phone, the phone goes in the coat pocket at wrap-up, not in your hand. If it is the last task, the last task moves to the evening before. If it is the shoe, the shoes go by the door the night before — every night, no exceptions. If it is the optimising, the fix is a reframe: fifteen minutes early is not fifteen minutes lost. It is fifteen minutes of sitting in a car with a podcast, which is a better use of your life than the specific cortisol of the last two miles.

That last one is the real block for perfectionists, who often turn out to be late for the same reason they are late with everything else. Worth reading on how to stop being a perfectionist if the pattern sounds familiar.

And when you are already going to be late — because sometimes you are — message before you are late, not after. "Running twelve minutes behind, sorry" sent at 8:50 costs you almost nothing. The same message at 9:10 costs you the reputation you were trying to protect.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why am I always late even when I try not to be?

Because trying harder does not change the number you are working from. Your estimate of how long the journey takes is built from your best ever run, not your average one, and it usually leaves out getting ready, parking, and walking. Effort applied to a wrong number just makes you rush. Measure the real door-to-door time once and use that instead.

Is chronic lateness a sign of ADHD?

It can be one feature of it, because difficulty judging how long tasks take is common in ADHD, but plenty of people who are late have nothing of the sort. Lateness alone is not a diagnosis. If it comes packaged with lifelong problems with focus, organisation and follow-through across every area of your life, that is worth raising with a doctor rather than settling with a blog post.

Does setting my clock fast actually work?

Not for long. You know you did it, so within a week or two you are automatically subtracting the ten minutes and you are back where you started. What works better is fixing the departure time itself, because it does not depend on you forgetting anything. Set an alarm labelled with the action — leave now — rather than lying to yourself about the time.

How long does it take to stop being late?

Faster than most habit changes, because it is a planning fix rather than a willpower one. Most people get a run of on-time arrivals within a week or two of doing the backwards plan for their regular journeys. The slow part is other people believing it. Give that a couple of months of consistency before you expect anyone to comment.

The Bottom Line

Being late is not a flaw in you. It is a flaw in a calculation you have never actually checked, repeated for years, defended by a brain that only remembers the fast version of every journey.

So check it. Time one journey properly, door to door. Plan one appointment backwards from the moment you need to be sitting down. Set one alarm that says LEAVE NOW instead of the name of the meeting. Move the one thing that always eats your last ten minutes to the night before.

That is the whole method for how to stop being late, and it is deliberately boring. It does not ask you to become more considerate or more disciplined. It asks you to use a real number instead of an optimistic one, which is the trade most people would take.
