How to Stop Feeling Overwhelmed (When Everything Is Too Much)

By Freddy Owen  ·  April 22, 2026  ·  10 min read

Feeling overwhelmed has a specific shape. You sit down to work and your brain goes blank. You open a list and close it. You check your phone, then forget why you opened it. A small task feels enormous. A normal day feels like a storm. If that sounds like your last week, you are not lazy. You are not broken. You are just running past the line your system can handle. The skill of how to stop feeling overwhelmed is not about trying harder. It is about learning how to drop the load long enough to think again.

This piece walks through what overwhelm actually is, why it hits some weeks and not others, and the short list of moves that actually calm it down. No magic. No crystals. Just practical steps you can run the next time your head starts to spin.

Overwhelm Is a Signal, Not a Weakness

The first shift, and probably the most important one, is this. Overwhelm is not a character flaw. It is a signal from your body that too much is stacked at once. Same way a check engine light is not the car being dramatic. It is the car telling you something it needs.

When you read overwhelm as weakness, you pile more pressure on top. Now you are not just behind on your life. You are also ashamed of being behind. That extra layer is what keeps people stuck. Drop it and you already have more room to move.

What is actually happening in your body

When the load gets too heavy, your nervous system kicks into a low-grade stress response. Your heart rate ticks up. Your breathing gets shallow. Your thinking narrows. This is useful if a bear is chasing you. It is a disaster if you are trying to write a report. The part of your brain that makes plans and breaks big things into small steps goes quieter. The part that panics gets louder.

So the first job, when you are overwhelmed, is not to plan better. It is to lower the alarm enough that your planning brain can come back online. Until you do that, nothing on your list will get done well anyway.

Why You Feel Overwhelmed by Things That Used to Feel Easy

One of the most confusing parts of overwhelm is how random it seems. Last month this exact task was fine. This week it feels like climbing a wall. What changed? Usually not the task. The load around the task.

Think of your system like a glass. Sleep fills it. Food fills it. Good company fills it. Work drains it. Conflict drains it. Worry drains it. Noise drains it. If the glass is already empty, even a tiny new thing can tip it. That is why you can cry over a misplaced sock on one day and laugh off a flat tire on another. The sock was not the problem. The glass was.

The hidden drains

People usually know the loud drains. A big deadline. A sick parent. A bad breakup. They miss the quiet drains that often do more damage because they just keep going.

  • Poor sleep for more than a few nights in a row
  • Too many small decisions, like every meal and every email
  • Background conflict, even conflict you have not named yet
  • A phone that never lets your brain sit still
  • A to-do list with items that have been there for months
  • Open tabs and open loops, both on your computer and in your head

Fixing one of these often does more for overwhelm than a new planner ever will. If you have a low-grade sense of too much even on a “light” week, the quiet drains are almost always the answer.

How to Stop Feeling Overwhelmed in the Moment

When overwhelm is hitting hard, you do not need a five-year plan. You need a ninety-second reset. Here is the short list, in order, for when the chest gets tight and the to-do list starts to blur.

1. Slow the breath with a long exhale

The fastest way to drop the alarm in your body is a breathing pattern with a longer out-breath than in-breath. Try four seconds in through the nose, six or eight seconds out through the mouth. Do it for ninety seconds. Your heart rate will slow. Your thinking will widen back out. You will not feel great. You will just feel a little less like you are going to fly apart.

This sounds too simple to work. It is not. It works because you are not arguing with your body. You are speaking to it in a language it actually listens to.

2. Get it out of your head and onto paper

Overwhelm often shows up as a swirl of “everything.” Until you name the everything, your brain has to keep holding all of it at once. That is what makes it feel so heavy.

Grab any piece of paper. Dump everything you are worried about in a fast, ugly list. No order. No categories. Laundry next to contract renewal next to your kid’s birthday. Just get it out. This is called a brain dump and it is the single fastest overwhelm cure most people have never tried. Your head gets lighter within a minute or two because the paper is now holding the list, not you.

3. Circle one small thing and start

On the dumped list, circle one thing. Not the most important one. The smallest, easiest one you can finish in ten minutes. Reply to one email. Start one load of laundry. Drink a glass of water. The point is not to make progress on the big list. The point is to break the freeze. Motion comes back first, not willpower.

Ninety-second rule: when overwhelm hits, breathe for ninety seconds with a long exhale before you touch anything else. It is the cheapest fix in the book and the most underused.

How to Shrink a Mountain of Work

Sometimes the overwhelm is not about a bad week. It is about a real pile. The tax stuff. The move. The project that grew three sizes. In those cases, the trick is to learn how to shrink the mountain instead of climbing it all at once.

The one-tab rule

Your brain can only really do one thing at a time. Not two. Not “multitask.” One. Every extra tab, window, app, or open loop is a small tax on your focus. Multiply that tax across a day and you feel exhausted after accomplishing nothing.

Try the one-tab rule for a day. Close every tab and app that is not the single thing you are doing right now. Phone face down in another room. When the next task starts, close the last one. Most people who try this for a full day cannot believe how much lighter their head feels by 3pm. That weight was overwhelm in disguise.

Shrink tasks until they are silly

A task you are avoiding is usually too big. Not in real size. In the size you have it stored in your head. “Do taxes” is a huge task. “Open the tax folder” is a three second task. Start with the three second version. Once you start, the next step shows up on its own. Overwhelm lives in “do taxes.” It dies in “open the folder.”

Write every task on your list with the very first action, not the goal. Not “plan birthday.” “Text three friends to ask if Saturday works.” Not “clean out garage.” “Put one empty box by the door.” The Goal Tracker works well here if you want it structured. You can start one at the Goal Tracker.

Let some balls drop on purpose

If your load is real and not imagined, no system will make all of it fit. The math just does not work. At that point, the job is not better organizing. It is choosing what does not get done this week on purpose. If you do not choose, your brain will keep every single thing on the list alive in the background, and you will pay for all of it in overwhelm.

Once a week, look at your list and mark three items you are letting slide. Not forever. Just for now. The calm comes from the choice, not from finishing everything. If this connects to something bigger, how to stop being a perfectionist goes deeper on the part that cannot let anything drop.

Long-Term Fixes for Constant Overwhelm

If you are overwhelmed most days and not just on hard weeks, you need more than a breathing trick. You need to look at the shape of your life and change a few structural things.

Protect one real pause a day

Not a phone break. A real pause. Twenty minutes where you are not producing, not consuming, not problem-solving. A walk with no podcast. Lying on the floor. Sitting in a parked car. Your nervous system needs these on-ramps to recover. Without them, overwhelm becomes the default setting, not the exception.

Review your yeses

Overwhelmed people usually have a yes problem. Every yes you give is a debit from your week. If you say yes to everything, you cannot be surprised that the account is empty by Thursday. Look at the last month. Which yeses drained you most? Which ones were kind of a shrug? Put a quiet no in the rotation and watch overwhelm thin out. The people-pleasing angle on this is covered in how to stop people pleasing.

Sleep is not optional

Most people treat sleep like a bonus. It is not. When sleep drops below seven hours for a string of nights, your ability to handle normal load takes a cliff dive. If you want one thing that would change overwhelm more than any app, productivity system, or self-help book, it is getting sleep back in range. Everything else works better once that one is handled.

Track the load, not just the tasks

Most planners track tasks. They do not track the total weight. A useful habit is a two-minute end-of-day note. Energy level today, out of ten. One thing that filled the glass. One thing that drained it. After a few weeks you will start to see patterns. You will know which Tuesdays to defend. You will know which meetings actually cost you. The Journal works for this kind of light tracking.

When Overwhelm Turns Into Something Bigger

Regular overwhelm is uncomfortable but fine. It passes when the load changes. There is another kind, though, where the overwhelm stops going away. Weeks of it. Months. Even rest does not reset it.

At that point, you are not dealing with a time management problem. You are likely dealing with burnout, long grief, unresolved anxiety, or something that needs more care than a brain dump can give. The signs to watch for are these. Tired that sleep does not fix. Losing interest in things that used to matter. Basic tasks feeling like boulders. A quiet sense of hopelessness underneath the stress.

If that is where you are, please do not try to self-help your way through it alone. Talk to a doctor. Talk to a therapist. Talk to a friend who will not rush you. There is no gold star for handling it by yourself. For related ideas on the stress side of this, see how to deal with stress.

A One-Week Reset for Overwhelm

If you want a simple reset you can run this week, here is the whole thing.

Day 1. Brain dump in the morning. Circle one tiny task. Do it. Protect a twenty minute pause with no screen in the afternoon.

Day 2. Run the one-tab rule all day. Phone in another room during your most focused hour.

Day 3. Look at your yeses for the week. Cancel one that is a drain. Do not replace it.

Day 4. Sleep is the job today. Bed thirty minutes earlier than usual. No late caffeine. See how different Friday feels.

Day 5. Pick three items off your list and formally drop them for the week. Write them down as dropped, not as pending.

Day 6. One real pause. One long walk. No productivity on the list at all.

Day 7. Review. Which moves lowered the glass the most? Keep those. Drop the rest.

One week will not fix a life. It will show you which levers work for your system, which is more useful than any generic plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I feel overwhelmed by small things?

You feel overwhelmed by small things because your system is already running near its limit. The small thing is not the cause. It is the last straw. When sleep, food, and mental load are off, the same task that was easy last month feels impossible this week. The fix is rarely the task itself. It is the load around the task.

What is the quickest way to calm down when overwhelmed?

The quickest way to calm an overwhelmed body is slow breathing with a long exhale, for about ninety seconds. Four seconds in, six or eight seconds out. Then drink a glass of water. Then write down everything swirling in your head in a quick list. You are not solving anything yet. You are lowering the alarm so your brain can think again.

How do I get started when I have too much to do?

You pick one task, make it small, and do it for ten minutes. That is the whole move. Overwhelm freezes you because the brain is staring at the whole pile. Pick one item. Shrink it until it is almost insultingly easy. Set a ten minute timer. Finish when the timer ends, even if you feel like going. The unfreezing is what you were after.

Is feeling overwhelmed a sign of burnout?

Feeling overwhelmed now and then is normal. Feeling overwhelmed for weeks, even after rest, can be a sign of burnout. Look for three markers. Tired that sleep does not fix. Losing interest in things you used to care about. Small tasks feeling like huge tasks. If all three are present, this is not a time management problem. It is a recovery problem, and you need real rest and probably support.

The Takeaway

Overwhelm is not a character flaw. It is a signal that the glass is too full. Drop the load before you touch the list. Breathe slow with a long exhale. Dump the swirl onto paper. Pick the smallest thing on the page and start. For the bigger picture, guard your sleep, watch your yeses, and put one real pause in every day. You do not need a new system. You need a lighter glass. Start there this week, and the rest of your life gets a lot easier to carry.
