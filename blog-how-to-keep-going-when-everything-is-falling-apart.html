How to Keep Going When It Feels Like Everything Is Falling Apart

By Freddy Owen·October 2026

This post isn't for when one thing is hard. It's for when everything is hard â at the same time, all at once, in ways that feel too tangled to even start picking apart. The job, the relationship, the money, the health, the sense of direction. When you're not dealing with a problem but with a season that won't let up.

If that's where you are right now, I'm not going to insult you with "just stay positive." That kind of advice, delivered by someone who isn't in the middle of what you're in, isn't helpful â it's dismissive. So let's talk about what actually helps when the ground is shaking.

First: Acknowledge the Weight Without Drowning in It

There's a difference between naming what's hard and collapsing into it. Both matter. The first is honest and necessary. The second is where people get stuck.

One of the most damaging things you can do when everything is falling apart is to pretend it isn't. To push through on pure denial, telling yourself it's fine when it clearly isn't. That costs an enormous amount of energy â the energy of maintaining a fiction â and it prevents you from taking the actual problem seriously enough to address it.

But the opposite extreme â spending all your available mental and emotional resources on how bad things are â isn't useful either. It's not that feeling the weight is wrong. It's that staying in that feeling as the primary activity doesn't move anything.

The useful middle ground: name it, feel it for a real period of time, and then ask "okay, given this is where I am â what's one thing I can do today?"

You don't need to solve everything right now. You need to not make it worse, and take one small step forward. That's the whole job for today.

Why "Just Stay Positive" Is Actively Harmful

When things are genuinely falling apart, toxic positivity doesn't just fail to help â it can actively make things worse. Here's why.

If you've been told to "look on the bright side" of a situation that is objectively difficult, you haven't been helped. You've been told that your accurate perception of your situation is a problem. That your distress is the obstacle, not the thing causing the distress. That's backwards.

Forced positivity also tends to suppress the processing that needs to happen. When things go wrong, the brain needs to work through what happened, extract lessons, and update its model of the world. Covering that process with artificial optimism doesn't make it go away. It delays it, and delayed processing tends to come out sideways â as anxiety, irritability, or the kind of free-floating dread that doesn't seem attached to anything in particular.

What actually works is honest acknowledgment paired with forward motion. Not "everything's fine" but "this is hard, and here's what I'm going to do anyway."

The Smallest Viable Action

When everything is falling apart, most conventional productivity advice becomes useless. You can't "eat the frog" when you don't have the energy to identify which frog. You can't do your morning routine when you're running on disrupted sleep and genuine overwhelm.

What you can do is find the smallest viable action â the one thing that, if you did it today, would keep the thread intact. Not the most important thing on your list. The smallest thing that still matters.

Make one phone call. Send one email. Handle one small task that has been weighing on you. Get outside for ten minutes. Cook one real meal. The point is not that these small actions solve the problem. The point is that they prove to you that you can still function â that you're still capable of doing things, even when you feel incapable. That proof matters more than the action itself.

Why Small Actions Work When Nothing Else Does

Inertia is real in both directions. When you're stopped, starting is the hardest part. But once you're moving, even slightly, momentum builds. The act of completing one small task gives your brain a small dose of the reward signal it's been starved of during a hard season. That signal â even small â makes the next action slightly easier. And the next one easier still.

This is not about grinding through everything at full speed when you have nothing left. It's about staying in motion at whatever speed you can actually manage, so that when the season shifts â and it will shift â you haven't lost all your momentum and have to restart from zero.

Managing When the Problems Are Real and Multiple

Sometimes the difficulty isn't in your head. Sometimes the problems are real: the money situation is genuinely tight, the relationship is genuinely in trouble, the health situation is genuinely serious. I want to be honest about that.

What follows is not magic. It's not a system that makes real problems disappear. But these approaches help when you're facing several real problems at the same time.

Triage, Don't Try to Handle Everything at Once

When multiple things are failing simultaneously, the instinct is to panic-manage all of them. That's understandable and also almost always the wrong move. Trying to give 30% to six problems typically produces worse outcomes than giving 80% to the two that matter most right now.

Triage your problems the way an emergency room triages patients: what's life-threatening, what can wait, what will get worse if left alone, what can hold for a week. Not everything that feels urgent is urgent. And not everything that's important can be addressed right now. Be ruthless about this. Trying to handle everything is how you handle nothing.

Narrow Your Time Horizon

When you're overwhelmed, projecting forward into the future becomes terrifying. All the ways things could continue to be bad, or get worse, or spiral. That projection is largely your brain trying to prepare for threats â a useful function at the right scale, a destructive one when you're already maxed out.

Narrow the time horizon. Not forever â just for now. Stop asking "what is my life going to look like in a year if this continues?" and ask "what am I doing today?" Then ask "what am I doing this week?" That's it. A year-sized problem is too big to hold. A day-sized version of it is manageable.

Protect One or Two Anchors

In a hard season, almost everything can flex. But there are usually one or two anchors â things that, if you protect them, keep you functional enough to deal with everything else. Sleep is the most common one. A basic level of physical movement. One relationship where you can be honest about what's going on.

Identify your anchors. Guard them like they're the infrastructure â because they are. When the anchors go, the whole thing gets harder. When the anchors hold, you have just enough stability to work with what you've got.

Hard Seasons End â And What They Leave Behind

I want to say something I mean, not something I say because it sounds good: hard seasons end. Not immediately, not on your timeline, not always cleanly â but they end. And the people I've watched come through genuinely difficult periods don't come out unscathed. They come out changed.

The ones who managed the hard season well â who stayed in motion, kept their anchors, didn't catastrophize, asked for help when they needed it â they come out with something the smooth periods don't build: actual resilience. Not the idea of resilience. The experience of having gone through something hard and discovered that you could.

That discovery changes what you're afraid of going forward. It changes how quickly you recover from the next hard thing, because you have proof that you've recovered before. It changes the stories you tell yourself about what you can handle. And those stories, as I keep saying, matter enormously.

Hard seasons are also where you find out who's actually in your corner. Who shows up, who disappears, who can handle honesty and who only wanted the easy version of you. That information is useful even when acquiring it is painful.

Character gets built in the hard seasons, not the easy ones. The easy ones are good â enjoy them. But this is where the real work of becoming who you want to be actually happens.

When to Ask for More Help Than This

I'll be straight here too. If you're in a season where "everything is falling apart" has become a sustained state â months, not days â and you're consistently unable to function, that's not something a blog post fully addresses. That's a situation where talking to someone qualified â a therapist, a counselor, a doctor â is the right call, not a last resort.

There's no shame in needing more support than you currently have. Asking for that support is itself one of the harder things to do, which makes it one of the more genuinely courageous ones. The people who come through hard seasons consistently are not the ones who white-knuckled it alone. They're the ones who asked for help at the right moment.

You're the only one who knows what you're actually carrying.

Keep going. Not because it's easy, but because you can.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you keep going when you're exhausted?

You reduce the ask. When you're running on empty, the goal isn't to match your best performance â it's to keep the minimum viable thread going. Do the smallest possible version of what matters most. One email. One task. Ten minutes. The goal is continuity, not peak performance. Continuity keeps you in the game until you have more capacity.

Is it okay to take a break when things are hard?

Yes â and often strategically important. A genuine break, even a short one, can restore enough capacity to keep going. The key is the difference between a deliberate pause and checking out completely. A deliberate pause has a defined length and a clear re-entry point. It's a resource management decision, not a surrender.

What do you do when you lose motivation completely?

Stop waiting for motivation to return. When everything is hard, motivation is usually the last thing that comes back. Action comes first. Start with the smallest possible action â one that requires almost no motivation â and let the momentum of doing something pull you forward. Motivation follows action; it rarely precedes it in hard seasons.

How do successful people handle setbacks?

The most consistent pattern isn't that they handle setbacks better in the moment â it's that they have a clearer relationship with difficulty. They expect hard periods to happen. They don't treat difficulty as evidence that something has gone wrong with them. And they've built the habit of asking "what's the next right thing" rather than looking for the exit when things get uncomfortable.
