How to Stop Doomscrolling (and Take Your Time Back)

By Freddy Owen · August 5, 2026 · 7 min read

You picked up your phone to check one thing. That was forty minutes ago. Now you are deep in a stranger's vacation photos, a few bad headlines heavier, and somehow more tired than when you started. If you want to know how to stop doomscrolling, the first thing to accept is that you are not weak and you are not broken. You are using a product built by very smart people to keep you doing exactly this.

Doomscrolling is the endless, joyless scroll through bad news, social feeds, and other people's highlight reels. It rarely makes you feel better. Most of the time it makes you feel worse. And yet you keep going, sometimes for hours, often without ever deciding to.

This post is not going to tell you to "just put the phone down." If that worked, you would have done it already. Instead we are going to look at why the habit has such a grip on you, and the specific changes that actually loosen it.

Why You Can't Stop Doomscrolling

The feed never ends. That is not an accident. Older media had a stopping point — the paper ended, the show finished, the page ran out. An infinite feed removes every natural place to stop, so the decision to quit has to come entirely from you, over and over, against an app designed to make sure it never does.

On top of that, every scroll is a small gamble. Most posts are boring, but every so often there is something funny, shocking, or useful. Your brain does not know which scroll will pay off, so it keeps pulling the lever. This is the same mechanism that makes slot machines addictive, and it is baked into your phone habit whether you notice it or not.

Bad news hooks you harder

There is a reason the "doom" is in doomscrolling. Your brain is wired to pay extra attention to threats. A calm, reassuring headline barely registers. A frightening one grabs you and holds on. Feeds learn this fast and serve you more of what keeps you staring, which usually means more of what makes you anxious.

So you are not scrolling because you have no willpower. You are scrolling because the machine is very good at its job. Once you see it as a designed trap instead of a personal failing, you can start to fight it on the right terms.

The Real Cost of Doomscrolling

It is easy to shrug this off as a harmless way to kill time. It is not harmless, and the cost shows up in two places most people never connect back to their phone.

It quietly raises your baseline anxiety

When you spend an hour absorbing crises you cannot do anything about, your body reacts as if those threats are in the room with you. Your nervous system does not know the difference between a real danger and a video of one. Do this every day and you walk around with a low hum of dread you cannot explain. If you already struggle to deal with anxiety, doomscrolling pours fuel on it.

It steals time you never notice losing

Twenty minutes here, thirty minutes there. It feels like nothing in the moment. Add it up and it is often two, three, four hours a day — a part-time job's worth of your one life, spent on a feed you will not remember tomorrow. That is time you could put toward the things you say you never have time for. Being honest about that number is the first push toward wanting it back. Our Time Wasted calculator will show you the real total, and it usually stings.

How to Stop Doomscrolling by Fixing Your Environment

Willpower is a terrible long-term strategy. You have a limited amount of it, and the app has infinite patience. The reliable way to reduce screen time is to change your environment so the phone is harder to grab and the feed is harder to reach. Make the bad habit inconvenient and half the battle is already won.

Add friction between you and the feed

Delete the worst apps off your phone and use them only in a browser, where they are clunky on purpose. Log out so you have to type your password every time. Turn your screen to grayscale so the feed loses its candy-colored pull. Move the apps off your home screen and into a folder on the last page. None of these are dramatic, but each one adds a second of friction, and that second is often enough to break the automatic reach.

Protect the danger zones

Most doomscrolling happens at predictable times: first thing in the morning, in bed at night, and any moment you feel bored or stressed. Guard those windows. Charge your phone outside the bedroom so it is not the first and last thing you touch each day. Buy a cheap alarm clock so you have no excuse to keep it on the nightstand. These small structural changes beat any amount of promising yourself you will do better, and they are the same tactics that help you stop wasting time on your phone in general.

Rewire the Habit, Not Just the Settings

Settings help, but the habit lives deeper than your home screen. Every habit runs on a loop: a cue, a routine, and a reward. The cue is usually a feeling — boredom, loneliness, stress, or just a gap in your day. The routine is reaching for the phone. The reward is a few seconds of distraction from whatever you did not want to feel.

You cannot delete the cue. You are going to feel bored and stressed no matter what. But you can swap the routine. When you notice the urge, name it: "I am bored," or "I am avoiding that email." Just naming it breaks the trance for a second, and a second is all you need to choose something else. If you want to go deeper on interrupting mental loops, the same skill helps you stop overthinking too.

Then give yourself an easy replacement ready to go, so you are not choosing between the feed and staring at a wall. This is the same principle behind building any better routine: make the good option the easy one. You can track the swap with the Habit Builder and watch the streak grow, which turns "stop doomscrolling" into something you can actually see yourself winning.

What to Reach For Instead

Telling yourself to stop is only half the job. Your hand reaches for the phone because it has somewhere to go. Give it somewhere better. Keep a real book on the nightstand instead of the phone. Put a water bottle or a notebook where the phone used to sit. When the urge hits, do one push-up, step outside, or text an actual friend instead of watching strangers.

The point is not to fill every second. Boredom is not the enemy — it is where a lot of good thinking and creativity come from. Part of learning how to stop doomscrolling is getting comfortable with a quiet, unstimulated mind again, instead of numbing it the moment it appears. The more you sit with a little boredom, the less power the feed has over you, and the easier it gets to focus on things that matter.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is doomscrolling so hard to stop?

Because it is engineered to be. Infinite feeds remove any natural stopping point, and the unpredictable mix of interesting and boring posts triggers the same reward system as gambling. On top of that, your brain is wired to fixate on threats, so bad news holds your attention harder than good news. You are not fighting a lack of willpower, you are fighting a system built by professionals to keep you scrolling.

How much time does the average person spend doomscrolling?

It varies, but many people lose two to four hours a day to their phones without realizing it, and a large chunk of that is passive scrolling rather than anything useful. The scary part is how invisible it is — twenty minutes at a time never feels like much. Track it for a few days and the real number usually shocks people into wanting to change.

Does doomscrolling actually cause anxiety?

It strongly feeds it. When you flood your mind with alarming information you cannot act on, your nervous system responds as though the danger is real and present. Do that daily and you carry a constant low-grade stress you may not even trace back to your phone. Cutting down almost always calms that hum within a week or two.

What is the fastest way to stop doomscrolling?

Add friction today: delete the worst apps from your phone, log out of the rest, and charge your phone outside your bedroom tonight. You will not scroll what you cannot reach in one tap. Then give your hand something else to grab. It is not about willpower, it is about making the habit inconvenient.

The Bottom Line

You are not addicted because you are weak. You are hooked because the feed was built to hook you. That means the way out is not shame or more willpower — it is design. Make the phone harder to reach, swap the routine when the urge shows up, and keep something better within arm's length.

Pick one change from this post and do it in the next five minutes. Log out of one app, or move your charger out of the bedroom tonight. Then use the Habit Builder to track the days you keep the feed at bay. Get enough of them in a row and you will not have to fight the urge anymore. You will just be someone who does not doomscroll.
