How to Find Your Core Values (Free Quiz + Step-by-Step Guide)

By Freddy Owen · October 17, 2026 · 8 min read

Most people have never seriously thought about their core values — and it shows. They make career decisions based on salary, relationship decisions based on availability, and life decisions based on what's expected of them. Then they wonder why, even when things go well by measurable standards, something still feels off. Knowing your core values doesn't fix everything. But it does make decisions clearer, drift more obvious, and the feeling of being stuck much easier to diagnose. Here's how to find yours.

Why Knowing Your Core Values Matters

Values are the criteria by which you — consciously or not — judge whether a life is being well-lived. When your daily life aligns with your values, things feel meaningful and energising even when they're hard. When your daily life conflicts with your values, things feel draining and hollow even when they're objectively going well.

That misalignment is one of the most common sources of the vague dissatisfaction people can't quite name. The job pays well and looks impressive on paper, but it feels empty. The relationship is stable and comfortable, but there's something missing. The life looks right from the outside. It just doesn't feel right on the inside. Usually, the reason is a values conflict — you're living in a way that contradicts what actually matters to you at a fundamental level.

When you know your core values with real precision, two things happen. First, decisions get easier. When a choice aligns with your values, you feel drawn to it. When it conflicts, you feel the resistance. Second, drift becomes visible immediately. You can look at your current commitments and time allocation and instantly see where there's a gap between what you say matters and where you're actually putting your life. That visibility is power.

The Problem with Most Values Lists

If you've done a values exercise before and found it useless, this is probably why: the lists of values you're given are full of generic words — integrity, excellence, growth, family — that sound meaningful in the abstract but are completely hollow in practice because everyone chooses them.

When someone says their top values are integrity, growth, and family, that tells you almost nothing about how they make decisions or what trade-offs they're willing to make. Does "growth" mean intellectual learning or financial advancement? Does "family" mean prioritising time at home or building generational wealth? Does "integrity" override "loyalty" when they conflict?

The problem isn't the words. It's that values only become meaningful when they're specific enough to actually guide a decision — especially when two values conflict. Your real values are revealed by what you choose when you can't have everything. That's the test.

Step 1: Identify Your Peak Experiences — Times You Felt Fully Alive

The most reliable way to find your genuine values is to look backwards at your experience rather than picking from a list. Start by recalling three to five moments in your life when you felt most alive, most like yourself, most engaged. These could be professional or personal. Big moments or small ones. Recent or from years ago.

Write them down. Describe each one briefly: what were you doing, who were you with, what made it feel significant? Don't filter based on whether it seems serious enough. If you felt genuinely lit up during a conversation about ideas with a friend, that counts as much as a professional achievement.

Also do the inverse: recall three to five moments when you felt most drained, most resentful, most out of alignment — times when what you were doing felt fundamentally wrong or hollow. These negative experiences point to values violations as clearly as positive experiences point to values expression.

Step 2: Look for the Values Underneath Those Moments

Now examine your peak experiences and ask: what made each moment meaningful? What need was being met? What quality of experience was present that usually isn't?

Someone who felt most alive during a period of intense creative work might find autonomy and creativity underneath that experience. Someone who felt most alive coordinating a team through a crisis might find leadership and impact. Someone who felt most alive during deep one-on-one conversations might find connection and depth.

For your drained moments, ask: what was being violated? What was absent that you need? What were you being asked to compromise?

This reverse-engineering approach produces much more accurate values than any pick-from-a-list exercise because it's grounded in your actual experience rather than your aspirational self-concept.

Step 4: Narrow to Your Top 5 (And Why Fewer Is More Powerful)

After working through your experiences and the quiz, you'll likely have a list of eight to twelve values that resonate. Now you need to narrow it down. The goal is five — ideally three to five.

Here's why fewer is more powerful: if everything is a priority, nothing is a priority. A person with twelve core values has a framework that justifies almost any decision — which means it doesn't actually guide anything. A person with five core values has a framework that forces real choices. When two values conflict, the ranking matters. That's where the clarity lives.

To narrow your list, apply the conflict test: take two values from your list and ask, "If I had to choose between these in a real decision, which would win?" Run every value against every other value. The ones that consistently survive the tournament are your true top values.

Another approach: ask which values, if violated, would make you feel like you'd compromised yourself — dot just uncomfortable, but genuinely like you'd betrayed something essential. Those are the core ones.

Step 5: Test Your Values Against Your Current Life

Here's where it gets real. Take your top five values and hold them up against your actual life as it exists today. Ask, for each one:

  • Is this value expressed in how I spend my time?
  • Is it reflected in how I spend my money?
  • Is it present in my work, my relationships, my daily environment?
  • Would a stranger looking at my calendar and bank statement recognise this as a value of mine?

The gaps between your stated values and your lived reality are your action agenda. Not a reason for self-criticism — just information about where alignment work needs to happen.

What to Do When Your Life Doesn't Match Your Values

First: this is normal. Almost everyone discovers a gap. The goal of this exercise is not to find out that you're living in perfect alignment — it's to identify where the misalignment is so you can address it deliberately.

When you find a gap, don't try to change everything at once. Pick the single biggest misalignment — the value that's most important to you and most absent from your current life — and ask: what is one specific change I could make in the next 30 days that would bring my life a little closer to this value?

It doesn't have to be dramatic. If freedom is a core value and you have none at work, you don't necessarily need to quit your job tomorrow. You might start one project on the side that gives you creative control. You might negotiate one afternoon per week to work independently. Small expressions of core values, compounded over time, shift the character of a life.

The bigger misalignments — the wrong career, the wrong relationship, the wrong city — these often need to be addressed eventually. But they rarely need to be addressed all at once, and they're addressed most effectively from a foundation of smaller alignment wins, not in a panic.

Common Values and What They Really Mean

Here's a quick reference for some of the most commonly identified core values — with what they actually mean in practice rather than in the abstract:

  • Autonomy: The ability to direct your own work and life without requiring permission or approval. People high in autonomy often struggle intensely in bureaucratic environments regardless of compensation.
  • Connection: Deep, authentic relationships. Not social popularity, but the experience of being genuinely known by others. People high in connection feel hollow in environments that are transactional or superficial.
  • Achievement: The experience of accomplishing meaningful goals, particularly challenging ones. Not external recognition — the internal experience of mastery and progress.
  • Security: Stability, predictability, and the absence of threat. People high in security often underestimate how much their risk-aversion is a genuine value rather than a character flaw.
  • Impact: The sense that your efforts are making a meaningful difference to others. People high in impact feel purposeless doing work that doesn't connect to a visible positive effect on the world.
  • Creativity: The freedom and opportunity to generate new things — ideas, solutions, art, systems. Creativity as a value goes unsatisfied in highly rigid, routine, or execution-only roles.
  • Learning: The ongoing acquisition of new knowledge, skills, and perspectives. People high in learning become restless quickly in static roles and environments.
  • Integrity: Alignment between internal standards and external behaviour. Not abstract honesty, but the specific discomfort of acting in ways that contradict your own principles.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find my personal values?

The most reliable method is a combination of reflection on peak and low experiences (where were your values most expressed and most violated?) followed by a structured narrowing process to identify your true top five. The self-reflection approach is more accurate than picking from a list because it's grounded in actual experience rather than aspirational self-image. The quiz above is designed to do this efficiently and gives you specific, actionable output rather than generic virtue words.

What are the most common core values?

In studies of values across cultures, the most commonly reported core values include family, honesty, respect, achievement, independence, compassion, security, and creativity. But frequency doesn't equal importance. The most commonly chosen values are also the most socially desirable — and therefore the least diagnostic. What matters is which values are most specifically and consistently yours, not which ones rank highest in surveys.

How do values change over time?

Core values do shift — but more slowly than most people assume. Major life events (becoming a parent, serious illness, a significant loss) can shift core values meaningfully. So can sustained exposure to a radically different environment. But the values that felt most essential in your twenties are generally still recognisable in your forties, even if their expression has changed. What tends to change more than the values themselves is your clarity about them — and your willingness to act on them.

What if I don't know what my values are?

That's what this exercise is for. Most people haven't thought about their values explicitly — they've just been making decisions and feeling whatever they feel about the results. If the reflection process doesn't surface anything clear, look at your strong reactions: what makes you feel genuinely angry, genuinely inspired, genuinely resentful? Those emotional signals are values signals. Strong reactions point to values, almost without exception.

Your Move

Most people go their whole lives making decisions based on an operating system they've never consciously examined. Getting clear on your core values is the operating system upgrade that everything else in personal development is built on. Start with the reflection process — write down your peak moments, look for the values underneath them — then take the free Core Values Quiz to sharpen the picture. Then hold those values up against your current life and identify the biggest gap. That gap is where the work is. And that work, done consistently, is what actually changes a life.
